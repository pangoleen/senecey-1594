ENGLISH TRANSLATION
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Sir, I have told you so many times how things stand at this court, where I still see no change,
that I think you have by now taken some decision. To build empty hopes in the mind is to deceive
oneself. We have shown the illness to those from whom we want the cure, but none is so deaf as he
who will not hear. At last, because I think affairs press, I gave the Pope the strongest account
I could of our ills. I told him freely that we could bear them no longer, and that there were
three ways to free us. The first, the one most wanted by my lord of Mayenne and by many good men,
was the ruin of our enemies. We found many difficulties in it, given the weak help that we have
had until now. If His Holiness thought it right that my lord should spend the rest of his friends
and his life, he wished for no more honourable tomb than the one made ready for him on a
battlefield, fighting for the glory of God and to carry out the commands of His Holiness. But his
certain loss, and with it the loss of religion in France, would bring little profit to those who
caused it. Everyone had to be shown clearly that our cause prospered. Nothing would hold men's
loyalty better than strong and quick help, with the holy blessings of His Beatitude and some
millions of his money added to the help of Spain (which was thought larger than before). If that
could not be done, all the Catholics would turn to the second way, the one most commonly wanted:

[[ that His Holiness should be pleased to take in hand the general pacification of all
Christendom, and by his authority bring to France the securities that are needed for the Catholic
religion and for those who have upheld it, and in particular bring to my lord the honourable marks
that his labours have justly earned. His Holiness would reach this easily if he sent a legate to
the place, assisted by the French cardinals. This good intention would be followed and embraced by
all the Catholics in general, and no less by those who attend ]] "le fin" (?) [by the sense, Henri
of Navarre], who fear [[ his establishment by force as much as those of the Union do, ]] because
both sides fear [[ that, if this came to pass (which God forbid), he would use or abuse religion
as he pleased. ]] And if

neither of these two ways took effect quickly, it was beyond doubt that [[ each man would think
of his own affairs. ]] The great [[ towns that depend on themselves are so weary and ruined that
they will treat with the said Navarre; and ]] those that are [[ commanded by governors and
garrisons, ]] who no longer find the means [[ to keep their soldiers, will do the same; ]] for
[[ the said Navarre has his arms open ]] to receive the [[ peoples, and his hands wide ]] to give
large rewards to the [[ governors and private men who bring him good strongholds. ]] This has
been seen and done only too often already, and every man will do the same if [[ the hope were
wholly lost that His Holiness ]] would let himself be led to embrace the said [[ pacification.
The good men have held back until ]] now because they wish [[ to come out ]] of this bad
business [[ with the authority of His said Holiness and security for the Catholic religion. ]]

You will use this account as the state of affairs requires when this letter arrives, and as a
friend. Given the bent of this court, it will always be easy to make it succeed in words. He [the
Pope] ordered me to give it to him in writing. I shall make no difficulty, because it holds
nothing unworthy of a servant of my lord, a Frenchman and a Catholic. If you are wise, good will
come to you of it and, as the proverb says, your children will be the better for it. For the
rest, Sir, I have been told here of the death of Monsieur de Candale. I trust in my lord's good
will, and I have asked the Pope to give my second son the abbey of Fontenay. He has granted it. I
go on to have the bulls drawn while I wait for my lord's letters of recommendation. I write to
him about them, and I humbly ask you to see that they are sent to me. Monsieur Renould (?) is my
banker. I remain, Sir, without ever changing, your faithful servant. Rome, 15 March 1594.

DOUBTS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
See NOTES.md, section "Open points", for the sign-level list.
1. "le fin" (f. 575r, clear word in cipher line 12). I read f-i-n. The sense needs the name of
   Henri of Navarre ("ledict Navarre" comes later). It can be a cover word. Not resolved.
2. "[qui] estoict": the cipher starts at "estoict". The relative is not written.
3. "autorita la France": the cipher has a-u-t-o-r-i-t-a-l-a. One a serves for "-e" and for "a".
4. "afaire[s]": the last sign reads r in pass A (afairer). An s sign is expected.
5. "mesmes" (f. 575v line 4): a T-like stroke is joined to the sign for m. I take it as a pen flourish.
6. "seurtte": one t sign or two (seurte or seurtte).
7. Eight line ends of f. 575v are in the gutter of the binding. One or two signs are lost at each:
   depanden[t], trai[c]teront, commande[es], mesme, pe[r]du, gen[s], qu[il]z, d[e], c[at]holicque.
   The sense restores them.
8. Clear text: "veu (?)", "lieu (?)", "Renould (?)", "sages" are single-pass readings of the
   secretary hand.
